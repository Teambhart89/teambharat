The three checks that come before the design

A terrace garden that goes wrong rarely goes wrong because of the plants. It goes wrong because water sat somewhere it should not have, or because the slab was carrying more than anyone calculated. These checks take a morning and they save a great deal.

Waterproofing

We look for existing damp patches on the ceiling below and at the condition of the current treatment. If the terrace already has sound waterproofing and no history of seepage, we work on top of it. If there is any sign of a problem, it gets fixed first.

Why the order matters

Retrofitting waterproofing under an established terrace garden means dismantling all of it. Doing it before is a scheduled job. Doing it after is a demolition.

Drainage

Where does the water go in a heavy monsoon shower, and does anything in the new layout block that path. Standing water is what damages roofs and breeds mosquitoes, and both problems are designed out rather than treated later.

Load

Wet soil is heavy, and it is heaviest exactly when the slab is already dealing with rain. We keep the weight over beams and columns rather than mid span, use lightweight growing medium instead of garden soil, and prefer fibre and FRP planters to concrete. Where a client wants mature trees or a water feature, we ask for a structural opinion before designing around them.

Designing a terrace you will actually use

The most common regret we hear is a terrace filled edge to edge with planting and nowhere comfortable to sit. Green space is only worth the money if someone spends time in it.

Leave room for people

We start by marking where seating goes and how people move through the space, then plant around it. It produces a smaller plant list and a much better terrace.

Containers, raised beds or a mix

Containers are flexible, easy to move and simple to drain, and they suit rented properties and anyone who might change their mind. Raised beds hold more soil, support larger plants and look more permanent, and they need a proper root barrier and drainage layer beneath them. Most terraces we build use both.

Shade and screening

A Delhi terrace in May is unusable without shade, and shade is what makes the difference between a garden you look at and one you sit in. Pergolas, shade netting over a section, and tall planting on the western edge all work. So does simply accepting that summer afternoons are indoors and designing for mornings and evenings.

Wind on high floors

Terraces above the eighth floor or so get wind that flattens tall plants and dries containers out fast. Lower, denser planting and heavier planters handle it. Trellis screening on the exposed side helps more than people expect.

Plants for a Delhi NCR terrace or balcony

Which direction the space faces decides more than any other factor. Get this right and most other choices become easy.

Full sun, west or south facing

Bougainvillea, adenium, portulaca, hardy succulents, curry leaf, lemon grass and hibiscus. These handle the heat that flattens ordinary decorative foliage through May and June.

Partial sun, east facing

The easiest position to plant. Most flowering and foliage plants do well, including jasmine, ixora, and a wide range of seasonal colour.

Shade, north facing or covered

Ferns, aglaonema, syngonium, money plant, peace lily and snake plant. A shaded balcony is not a limitation, it is simply a different plant list, and often a lower maintenance one.

Terrace kitchen gardens

Tomato, chilli, spinach, methi, coriander, brinjal and beans all crop well in containers here. Winter is the productive season in NCR and summer output drops sharply, so most terrace kitchen gardens run hard from October to March and tick over with herbs under a shade net through the hot months.

Container depth for vegetables

Leafy greens manage in six to eight inches of depth. Tomatoes, brinjal and anything root heavy want twelve or more. Trying to grow the second group in the first group's container is the usual reason a terrace vegetable patch disappoints.

Watering without being tied to the terrace

A drip line on a simple timer solves watering for most terraces and is worth installing even when someone is normally home, because it waters at the right time of day rather than whenever you remember. Self watering planters are a reasonable alternative on balconies where running a line is not practical.

Containers dry out far faster than ground beds, especially in wind and sun. A terrace garden without a watering plan is a terrace garden with a two week failure point built into it.

What happens after setup

A balcony is often finished in a day or two. A full terrace with planters, planting and a watering line usually runs three to ten days on site, longer where decking or carpentry is involved, and waterproofing work adds its own curing time before we can start.
